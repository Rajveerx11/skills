# Paper Deck Style

Build a calm, high-end deck where each slide makes one claim and one visual proves or explains it. Work directly on the Paper board when a connected Paper tool is available.

## Discover before designing

Inspect:

- board dimensions, slide order, section boundaries, and nearby finished slides;
- repeated grid, margins, title scale, body scale, palette, footer, corner radius, stroke, and image treatment;
- audience, presentation setting, desired decision, source material, and factual constraints;
- reusable board components, screenshots, diagrams, and brand assets.

Create a compact internal **deck fingerprint**: grid, type hierarchy, spacing rhythm, palette roles, graphic language, and transition pattern. On an existing deck, this fingerprint is the default source of truth.

Ask only when audience, purpose, factual content, or permission to redesign cannot be inferred. Do not ask the user to choose abstract visual styles.

## Shape the story

1. Reduce source material to a sequence of claim headlines. Each headline must be a complete sentence or decisive phrase.
2. Assign one slide archetype to each claim: `opening`, `overview`, `section`, `explainer`, `comparison`, `proof`, `prompt-output`, or `close`.
3. Remove duplicate claims and split overloaded slides before styling.
4. Keep supporting detail in speaker notes or a follow-up slide; do not shrink it into unreadable copy.
5. Verify every number, quote, logo, customer claim, and attribution. Use placeholders only when clearly labeled.

For a new deck or explicit redesign, form three internal visual directions that differ in composition, typography, graphic device, and section rhythm. Select one using audience fit, story clarity, editability in Paper, brand truth, and visual distinction. Present options only when requested.

## Compose in Paper

- Use a slightly warm or brand-appropriate neutral background unless the deck fingerprint says otherwise.
- Favor one dominant visual, generous negative space, and short copy.
- Use editable Paper shapes, text, diagrams, and UI mockups when they communicate better than a raster image.
- Use real screenshots or source-backed diagrams. Never fabricate product UI, metrics, architecture, or testimonials.
- Reuse grid and alignment anchors across slides. Vary composition without changing the system.
- Reserve strongest contrast for the claim, proof, or action.
- Introduce section changes through one controlled shift: background tone, frame, label position, scale, or graphic motif.
- Preserve completed slides outside scope.

Read [references/slide-patterns.md](references/slide-patterns.md) when choosing or matching slide archetypes.

## Archetype defaults

- **Opening:** one promise, tension, or outcome; minimal supporting detail.
- **Overview:** three to five stages in one progression or cluster.
- **Section:** chapter claim plus a controlled system shift.
- **Explainer:** plain-language claim plus one metaphor, diagram, or annotated UI.
- **Comparison:** shared criteria and parallel structure; highlight the decisive difference.
- **Proof:** one result, chart, quote, or screenshot with source/context.
- **Prompt-output:** source on left, transformation in middle, verified result on right.
- **Close:** decision, next step, or memorable synthesis—not a generic thank-you.

## Efficient execution

1. Build a storyboard of slide headlines and archetypes.
2. Create one representative slide per visual family first.
3. Inspect those representatives, lock the fingerprint, then batch-build remaining slides.
4. Reuse components and alignments instead of recreating them slide by slide.
5. Render or screenshot the whole deck as a contact sheet when possible; inspect individual slides only where the contact sheet reveals risk.
6. Fix all findings in one batch, then confirm once.

## Artifact-level critique

Check the rendered deck:

- Can each slide's main idea be understood in three seconds?
- Does every visual earn its space and support the headline?
- Are type, contrast, and diagrams readable at presentation distance?
- Do adjacent slides vary enough to maintain attention while sharing one system?
- Are dense, orphaned, clipped, or off-grid elements visible?
- Does the full sequence build toward the requested decision?

Revise the weakest story or visual dimension once. Deliver board location, slides changed or added, sources/placeholders, and any unresolved factual input.
